# DAY III — Garden No One Owns

The soil-moisture probe knocked once against Salma's thermos when the train settled beside the agricultural platform.

She folded the deed along its old crease, put both objects in her coat pocket, and left the work compartment. Ivo remained at the table with the published record open on his reader. Beyond him, the empty identity fields were already fading into the ordinary grey of the page.

The stop was long enough for freight wagons to be detached. Salma crossed the platform while porters worked at the rear of the train. A covered path led from the station canopy to the greenhouse, its panes still wet where the morning condensation had begun to clear.

For two seasons, the civic gardening trust had used the land under a temporary licence. The permanent deed waited only for Salma's signature. She had inherited the unused strip beside the railway and had offered it for the greenhouse because nothing she intended to build there seemed as necessary.

She had also insisted on one protection.

**If future gardeners failed to maintain the land to her reasonable satisfaction, ownership would return to her.**

Inside, narrow beds ran between the glass walls. Seed trays occupied the warm tables; mature vines had been tied upward with pieces of cloth that did not match. No two labels used the same handwriting.

Salma switched on the probe and pushed it into the first bed. The reading appeared on her phone. The soil was within the range she had specified when the greenhouse opened.

The next bed was wetter. The one beside the service gate was too dry.

The trust coordinator followed her along the path.

“The irrigation line loses pressure at this end,” he said. “It will be replaced when the next maintenance grant arrives.”

“And if it doesn't?”

“We carry water.”

Near the far wall, two volunteers were doing exactly that. One held a watering can beneath a tap while the other moved seedlings away from a pane that had begun to admit too much heat.

Salma read the dry measurement again. The clause had seemed precise when the solicitor drafted it. Reasonable satisfaction could be supported by records: moisture, temperature, yield, opening hours, repairs. She had imagined data standing where trust might fail.

But the probe could establish only what the soil held at that moment. It could not say who should own the ground because a pipe had lost pressure, or how many neglected beds would make a future gardener unworthy of the land beneath them.

The coordinator opened the deed on a standing worktable. In addition to the reversion clause, the schedule reserved a permanent trustee's seat for Salma, a named entrance, and one planting bed whose use she could direct.

“Those can remain even if you soften the return condition,” he said. “The trust accepted them.”

“That isn't the same as needing them.”

Outside the glass, the detached freight wagons began moving along the siding. Their shadows crossed the beds one after another.

Salma selected the absolute transfer listed beneath the conditional one. It conveyed the land to the civic trust without a right of return. There was no donor seat, named entrance, reserved plot, approval power, or continuing standard enforceable by her.

The system displayed the differences: the return right, the trustee's seat, the entrance name, the reserved bed.

“If the greenhouse fails,” the coordinator said, “you cannot take the land back and try again.”

**“Then it will have a future I don't supervise.”**

She signed the unrestricted deed.

The filing receipt reached Enoch with the final instrument and its deletion history. The archive preserved what had been removed and when. It offered no opinion about whether the greenhouse would last.

Salma carried the probe to the dry bed and pushed it into the soil again. The number had not improved. She left the instrument on the potting shelf for the gardeners who would replace the line.

At the service gate, Oren was waiting beside an old boundary marker, a survey plan folded under his arm. The land now belonged to the trust, but the approach from the platform crossed a narrow railway strip. He would have to certify exactly where one ended and the other began.

Salma passed through the unlocked gate. Instead of drawing it closed behind her, she left it open toward the platform.

The train stood beyond it, ready to continue.

*Which line remains after the name beside it is erased?*
