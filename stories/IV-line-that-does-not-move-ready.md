# DAY IV — Line That Does Not Move

The self-leveling laser corrected itself with three small movements.

Then a red beam lay across the paving between the greenhouse gate and the railway fence, touching two survey marks that had been set into the concrete decades apart.

Oren checked both marks through the receiver. On one side stood the land Salma had transferred to the civic gardening trust. On the other was the station's service strip. Between them, the approach from the platform narrowed to the width of a delivery cart.

The gate Salma had left open was still open.

A fencing crew waited beside a stack of dark metal panels. Their work order treated the approach as railway maintenance access. Unless Oren filed the public coordinates before they began, the crew would place the new fence across the opening and leave a smaller locked gate for authorised staff. As the municipal surveyor assigned to the transfer, his registered plan would set both the access designation and the crew's alignment.

The developer's representative arrived with a revised drawing rolled beneath one arm.

His company owned the former packing warehouse beyond the greenhouse. It was willing to pay for the irrigation repair, five years of heating inspections, and maintenance of the platform approach. In exchange, the drawing closed the short approach from the platform to ordinary visitors and reopened it as the warehouse's sponsor entrance.

“The public can still enter from the rear road,” he said. “This would only be the direct gate.”

The public road curved behind the freight sheds. From the platform, it added nearly a kilometre.

“Who uses the direct gate?” Oren asked.

“Guests. Staff. People connected with the sponsor.”

“And everyone else?”

“Everyone else uses the rear entrance.”

On the drawing, the platform approach ended at the fence the crew was preparing to install.

The representative said this was only a sequencing problem. The maintenance agreement could be signed first; public access could be adjusted afterward. If Oren delayed certification until the parties had agreed, the company would fund the work immediately. The boundary itself, he assured him, would not move.

Oren looked along the laser beam. The statement was literally true. The cadastral line would remain where it had always been. What could move was the meaning attached to the narrow ground beside it: public approach, staff access, sponsor entrance, controlled gate.

One of the fencing workers lifted a drill from its case.

“Ten minutes,” the foreman said. “After that we lose the possession window.”

Oren set the receiver on the first survey mark and walked the beam to the second. He recorded the coordinates, the old monuments, the clear width to the platform, and the position of the open gate. He took a timestamped image from the railway side, then another from the greenhouse side.

The representative held out the revised plan.

“At least mark the line provisional.”

**"The measurement isn't provisional."**

“The agreement is.”

“Then the money can wait for the agreement.”

Oren uploaded the survey plan to the municipal register. Before submitting it, he added the designation required by Salma's unrestricted deed: **continuous public access from the platform approach to the trust entrance**.

The portal warned that filing would establish the access geometry before any maintenance or sponsorship arrangement. Later agreements could fund the route, but no private entrance could be added there without replacing the designation through a new public proceeding.

Oren stepped across the beam to the public side. The red line remained visible behind the toes of his boots.

He filed.

The registry returned a timestamp. A bounded copy went to Enoch containing the coordinates, the two images, and the public-access designation. It did not record the developer's intention or certify that future gates would remain open.

The foreman checked the updated work order. He stopped the drill, measured the registered width, and moved the first fence panel beyond the beam. The crew set it parallel to the railway strip, leaving the approach clear from the platform to the greenhouse gate.

Nothing dramatic crossed the boundary. A woman pushing an empty delivery cart came through first. She did not ask whose money would repair the irrigation.

The developer's representative withdrew the maintenance package from the project portal, then rolled up his drawing.

“Without an entrance, what would the sponsor receive?” he said.

Oren turned off the laser. The red beam vanished, but the fence remained where the filed coordinates had placed it.

At the next station, the municipal register routed the access instrument to Veda, the public notary assigned to certify it. An internal note asked her to hold the public reading until the sponsor announcement had been prepared.

She opened the filed plan and the request side by side.
